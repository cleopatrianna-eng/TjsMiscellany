The Secret Lives of Victorian Botanical Illustrators

Survey Exhibition · Various Venues

A magnificent survey of women whose meticulous brushwork shaped how an entire era understood the natural world — and whose names were routinely absent from the title pages of the books they made possible. The exhibition makes a compelling case not just for their artistry, but for the discipline of attention their work demanded.

Mucha: Art Nouveau and Utopia

Tate Modern, London

The exhibition argues, convincingly, that Alphonse Mucha was not merely a commercial artist — he was a man with an iconographic programme and a philosophical system. The Slav Epic, seen in reproduction, does not prepare you for the sheer physical scale of the originals.

A revelation: Mucha was always more than the posters suggested.

The Pre-Raphaelites: Obsession

National Gallery

A revisionist reading of the Brotherhood that frames their project as one of compulsive, sometimes unhealthy fixation. It works better than expected. Millais's Ophelia, seen across a crowded room, still stops you mid-step.

Stimulating reframing, even if the thesis slightly overreaches.
Craft & Design ★★★★★

William Morris: Anarchy and Beauty

V&A Museum

The great pleasure of this exhibition is its insistence on holding both sides of Morris together: the luxury wallpapers and the revolutionary politics, the exquisite handcraft and the rage at industrial capitalism. He is richer, and stranger, than either camp usually acknowledges.

Superb. The permanent collection has never seemed so important.
Illustration ★★★★☆

The Golden Age of Illustration

House of Illustration

Rackham, Nielsen, Dulac: the triumvirate of Edwardian fantasy illustration in one room. What strikes you, seeing these works in person rather than in reproduction, is the confidence of the line — these artists worked at speed, and it shows in the best possible way.

Worth visiting for the Nielsen watercolours alone.
Exhibition ★★★☆☆

The World in a Lens: Photography 1880–1920

National Portrait Gallery

A comprehensive survey that occasionally mistakes comprehensiveness for argument. There are marvellous individual images — particularly the ethnographic photographs, which come with welcome critical framing — but the whole never quite coheres into a vision.

Worthwhile for its parts; the sum is less than they deserved.
Craft & Design ★★★★★

Lucie Rie: Modernist Potter

Barbican Art Gallery

Rie's bowls are among the most beautiful objects made in the twentieth century — which is a bold claim, but one I am prepared to defend. In person, the surfaces have a quality that no photograph captures: a kind of luminous quietness that repays long looking.

Moving, precise, and profoundly satisfying.
