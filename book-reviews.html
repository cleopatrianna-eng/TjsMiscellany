Literature

Book Reviews

Considered opinions on fiction, non-fiction, and everything in between

Reviews are written without star ratings because books resist reduction. I write about why something matters, not merely whether it does.
Fiction ★★★★★

Piranesi

Susanna Clarke

A novel of such precise strangeness that one reads it with the peculiar sensation of inhabiting a dream that someone else is having. Clarke constructs a world that obeys its own logic with absolute rigour, and then populates it with a narrator of such guileless intelligence that the reader is perpetually two steps behind.

A genuine original: eerie, moving, and unlike anything else.
Non-Fiction ★★★★☆

The Cabinet of Curiosities

Horst Bredekamp

A scholarly and yet genuinely pleasurable account of the Wunderkammern — those Renaissance rooms of wonder where naturalia and artificialia jostled together before the age of classification divided them. Bredekamp writes with the enthusiasm of a collector and the precision of a historian.

Essential reading for anyone with a fondness for beautiful strangeness.
Fiction ★★★★☆

The Remainder

Alia Trabucco Zerán

An unsettling, formally inventive novel from Chile about children who grew up in the shadow of Pinochet's dictatorship and the particular burden of inherited trauma. Zerán's prose — in Lisa Dillman's extraordinary translation — is full of dark wit and a kind of feral grief.

Formally daring and morally serious. Highly recommended.
Classic ★★★★★

The Collected Stories

Mavis Gallant

To read Gallant is to understand what short fiction can actually do when in the hands of a supreme practitioner. These stories — mostly set in Europe, mostly about exile and displacement — are so precisely observed that they feel like recovered memories. The prose is entirely without waste.

One of the great short story writers in English. Read her.
Non-Fiction ★★★★☆

The Paper Trail

Alexander Monro

A history of paper — and therefore, in some sense, a history of civilization. Monro traces the material from its origins in China through its transformation of Europe and the Islamic world, never losing sight of the human stories that paper has carried and preserved.

Scholarly, surprising, and quietly profound.
Poetry ★★★★★

Night Sky with Exit Wounds

Ocean Vuong

A debut collection that reads like the work of a poet who has been writing for decades. Vuong writes about war, family, queerness, and America with a density of attention that makes each poem feel both inevitable and surprising. This is the kind of poetry that changes the reader.

Devastating and beautiful in equal measure. Essential.
